\title{Doxa and Logos: An Operator Model of the Interaction\\
between a Language Model and a Formal System}
\author{Vladimir Lapshin\thanks{\texttt{merfill@yandex.ru}}}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Large language models translate text into a formal language well, but they
cannot reason soundly: they fail to distinguish a correct step from a
plausible one, they do not track what an answer rests on, and they do not refuse to
guess. Symbolic solvers can do all of this, but they cannot read text. We
propose to treat this division not as an engineering compromise but as the
epistemological distinction between \emph{doxa} and \emph{logos}: doxa is a
source of proposals that have no derivation inside the system; logos is a
formal system that accepts or rejects them by strict rules. We introduce a
family of interaction operators between doxa and logos and distinguish three
roles within it: external (doxastic) operators supply a proposal not derivable
from the theory; internal (logotic) operators compute the system's reaction;
protocol operators record, classify, and control the exchange. The central
claim is that this interface is asymmetric. We show that the distinction has
rigorous formal analogues --- doxastic logic, non-monotonic logics, abduction
--- and that each of them describes one of the interaction operations. We also
describe a working system, Ankyra, in which a large language model plays the
role of doxa and a symbolic engine plays the role of logos.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Formal systems have a fundamental limit. G\"odel's incompleteness theorems
showed that any sufficiently rich consistent system contains statements it can
neither prove nor disprove \cite{godel}. Turing's halting problem showed that
no general algorithm exists that, for any program and input, would determine
whether it halts \cite{turing36}. These results are usually interpreted as
absolute limits of cognition.

We propose a different interpretation. These are the limits of \emph{static}
systems --- closed sets of axioms and inference rules. But human thinking is
not a static system. It does not begin with a fixed set of axioms and derive
consequences to exhaustion. It constantly expands, revises, and augments its
formalisms, relying on what the ancient tradition called \emph{doxa}.

The distinction between doxa and logos has a long history. Already in
Xenophanes (6th c.~BCE) \emph{doxa} ($\delta\acute{o}\xi\alpha$) denotes
\emph{opinion or belief}, fundamentally opposed to certain knowledge (truth).
Parmenides (5th c.~BCE) contrasts $\delta\acute{o}\xi\alpha$ with
\emph{truth} ($\grave{\alpha}\lambda\acute{\eta}\theta\epsilon\iota\alpha$):
doxa is the ``deceptive order'' of the sensory world, whereas truth is
accessible only to \emph{logos} --- pure thought that does not trust the
evidence of eyes and ears \cite{diels}. It is in Parmenides that the
distinction between the data of the senses and the data of logos becomes
radical: only the latter, on his view, is real and true.

Plato radicalized this distinction. For him, $\delta\acute{o}\xi\alpha$ is
cognition of \emph{what appears}, whereas \emph{episteme}
($\grave{\epsilon}\pi\iota\sigma\tau\acute{\eta}\mu\eta$) is cognition of
\emph{what is}. Doxa may be true or false, but it always remains
\emph{unstable}, \emph{finite}, \emph{contingent} --- it ``is expressed by
relation to an object, not by justification of truth'' \cite{plato}. Episteme,
by contrast, is knowledge \emph{accompanied by logos}, that is, by explanation,
justification, \emph{rigorous reasoning}.

In the \emph{Theaetetus}, Plato discusses and ultimately rejects the definition
of knowledge as ``true opinion with logos.'' This matters for us: logos is not
simply ``added'' to doxa; it works differently.

Aristotle then refined this: doxa is \emph{opinion}, which may be true but
lacks \emph{necessary} character; episteme is knowledge of \emph{the
necessary}, obtained through \emph{demonstration} \cite{aristotle}.

Thus, in the ancient tradition a stable distinction emerged: \emph{doxa} is
opinion, experience, probabilistic judgment rooted in the sensory and cultural;
\emph{logos} (or \emph{episteme}) is rigorous, justified, verifiable knowledge.
Doxa is \emph{wider}, but \emph{less reliable}. Logos is \emph{narrower}, but
\emph{guaranteed}.

Before identifying these terms with anything in a system, let us fix what
distinguishes doxa from logos. Not through negation (``doxa is not formalized
knowledge''), but through positive characteristics.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Historicity.} Experience accumulates in layers: the new does
	      not cancel the old but lies on top of it. Logos expands by adding
	      axioms, but it does not layer.
	\item \textbf{Associativity.} Doxa links the heterogeneous --- it sees
	      similarity where formally there is none. Logos links only what is
	      linked by inference rules.
	\item \textbf{Immediacy.} Doxa gives an answer without derivation, before
	      verification. Logos reaches an answer only through derivation.
	\item \textbf{Contextuality.} In doxa, meaning is determined by context.
	      In logos, meaning is fixed by the language $L$.
	\item \textbf{Probabilism.} Doxa gives a degree of confidence, not a
	      binary. Logos gives ``proven'' or ``not proven'', with nothing in
	      between.
\end{itemize}

These are characteristics of a type of knowledge, not of its bearer. They do
not claim anything about how doxa is ``really'' arranged; they fix how it
\emph{differs} from a formal system when it is regarded as a source of
proposals.

In this paper we use a large language model in the role of doxa. This is not a
metaphor and not an analogy: the LLM satisfies all five characteristics.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Historicity.} The LLM is trained on accumulated human text,
	      and its weights carry traces of many epochs and genres. Fine-tuning
	      adds layers without erasing previous ones.
	\item \textbf{Associativity.} Attention links tokens by statistical
	      proximity, not by inference rules. The model sees similarity where
	      formally there is none.
	\item \textbf{Immediacy.} The answer is produced in one direct pass,
	      without explicit derivation and without intermediate verification.
	\item \textbf{Contextuality.} The meaning of a token depends on the
	      entire context window; the same word in different contexts receives
	      a different representation.
	\item \textbf{Probabilism.} The output of the model is a probability
	      distribution over the next token, not a binary verdict.
\end{itemize}

It is precisely this correspondence that makes the identification meaningful.
If the LLM did not possess these characteristics, using it in the role of doxa
would be impossible, and the distinction would remain a philosophical metaphor.
It is not one.

The reason this identification becomes possible only now is that the formal
side has been developed for centuries: logic, proof theory, decision procedures,
specification languages. Logos was and remains the subject of exact research.
Doxa, by contrast, remained without an instrument: it was described
philosophically, but could not be addressed by a machine. The large language
model is the first instrument of this kind. That is why a distinction known for
two and a half thousand years becomes an \emph{architectural principle} only
now, rather than a philosophical metaphor.

\emph{Logos} in our system is a symbolic solver: a formal system that accepts
or rejects the proposals of doxa by strict rules. The distinction between doxa
and logos does not mean that one cancels the other. Human thinking relies on
both sources: doxa supplies content, logos verifies and holds it. In this paper
we are interested not in the nature of doxa as such, but in the
\emph{interface} between it and a formal system.

The key thesis of the paper is the following. Although doxa is not a theory in
the language of the formal system, \emph{the functions of its interaction with
that system} can be described formally. We introduce a family of interaction
operators and distinguish three roles within it: \emph{external} (doxastic)
operators supply a proposal not derivable from the theory; \emph{internal}
(logotic) operators compute the system's reaction to the proposal;
\emph{protocol} operators record, classify, and control the exchange. The fate
of the proposal is decided by logos --- it \emph{does not produce} it itself.

The paper is structured as follows. In Section~\ref{sec:theory} we examine the
theoretical foundations: the distinction between doxa and logos in the ancient
tradition and in modern logic, a survey of doxastic logic, non-monotonic
logics, abduction, and dynamic logics of belief. In
Section~\ref{sec:operators} we introduce the family of interaction operators
between doxa and logos and its formal properties. In
Section~\ref{sec:implementation} we describe a concrete implementation of this
model. In Section~\ref{sec:discussion} we discuss the limits of the approach,
its relation to hypercomputation, and practical implications for explainable
AI. Section~\ref{sec:related} concludes the paper.

\section{Theoretical foundations}
\label{sec:theory}

In Section~\ref{sec:intro} we introduced the distinction between doxa and
logos and showed its ancient origins. Here we will not repeat the history. Our
task is to show that this distinction has \emph{rigorous formal analogues} in
modern logic and that these analogues are \emph{relevant} to our model.

We consider three approaches: doxastic logic, non-monotonic logics, and
abduction. Each captures a separate aspect of the interaction between doxa and
logos. None of them describes doxa as such --- and this is not required: we are
interested not in the nature of doxa, but in those functions through which it
extends a formal system. These functions are what we describe in
Section~\ref{sec:operators}.

\subsection{Doxastic logic}
\label{sec:doxastic}

The term ``doxastic logic'' was introduced by \emph{Hintikka} in 1962 for the
formal modeling of an agent's \emph{opinions and beliefs}
($\delta\acute{o}\xi\alpha$), as distinct from knowledge
($\grave{\epsilon}\pi\iota\sigma\tau\acute{\eta}\mu\eta$) \cite{hintikka}. The
basic operator $B_\alpha$ reads as ``Alpha believes that\dots''. Doxastic logic
deals with \emph{synchronic} principles --- how beliefs are related to one
another at a given moment.

But it is \emph{silent} about how beliefs change over time. This limitation
was recognized, and the response to it was \emph{dynamic doxastic logic} (DDL),
introduced by \emph{Krister Segerberg} in the 1990s \cite{segerberg01}. DDL
adds to static doxastic logic \emph{modal operators of belief change}: $[*p]q$
reads as ``$q$ holds after revision of beliefs by $p$'' \cite{segerberg10}.

Thus DDL is a formal analogue not of doxa as such, but of the \emph{operator of
revision of the formal system} when a new proposal arrives from doxa. This is
precisely what we call a \emph{function of doxa}.

\subsection{Non-monotonic logics}
\label{sec:nonmonotonic}

Classical logic is \emph{monotonic}: if $\Gamma \vdash \phi$, then
$\Gamma \cup \Delta \vdash \phi$. Adding new axioms \emph{does not cancel}
previously derived consequences. But a formal system interacting with doxa
works differently. New experience may \emph{cancel} old conclusions. If I know
that Tweety is a bird, I assume he can fly. If I later learn that Tweety is a
penguin, I \emph{cancel} the conclusion ``Tweety flies,'' although it
previously followed from ``Tweety is a bird.''

\emph{Non-monotonic logics} are formal systems that model precisely this
\emph{defeasibility} of conclusions. Classical approaches include:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Default Logic} by \emph{Reiter}: rules of the form ``if $A$
	      and there is no information about exceptions, then $B$,'' which can
	      be defeated when an exception appears \cite{reiter}.
	\item \textbf{Circumscription} by \emph{McCarthy}: minimization of
	      ``abnormal'' situations, allowing default conclusions
	      \cite{mccarthy}.
	\item \textbf{Autoepistemic logic} by \emph{Moore}: modeling an agent's
	      beliefs about its own beliefs \cite{moore}.
\end{itemize}

All these formalisms acknowledge that \emph{the conclusions of a formal system
are not final}. They are \emph{defeasible} when new information from doxa
arrives. And this is not a ``deficiency.'' It is a \emph{property} of a system
that is \emph{open} to interaction with doxa. Non-monotonic logics formalize
this property but \emph{do not eliminate} it. They show how a system can
\emph{work} with defeasible conclusions without losing coherence.

\subsection{Abduction}
\label{sec:abduction}

If non-monotonic logics describe the \emph{cancellation} of conclusions, then
\emph{abduction} describes the \emph{generation} of new hypotheses. The term
was introduced by \emph{Charles Sanders Peirce} (1839--1914), who distinguished
three types of inference: deduction, induction, and \emph{abduction} ---
``the creative generation of explanatory hypotheses'' \cite{peirce_ru}.

Peirce characterized abduction as reasoning ``from consequence to cause.'' If
we observe a fact $Q$, and know that $P \to Q$, abduction allows us to
\emph{suppose} $P$ as a \emph{possible explanation} of $Q$. This is not
deduction (where $P$ \emph{necessarily} follows) nor induction (where $P$
\emph{probably} follows). It is the \emph{generation of a hypothesis} that
\emph{explains} the observation \cite{peirce_cp}.

In contemporary philosophy of science, abduction is often identified with
\emph{``inference to the best explanation''} (IBE). However, as researchers
note, \emph{the identification of abduction with IBE is not rigorous}. Peirce
distinguished the \emph{generation} of a hypothesis (abduction in the narrow
sense) from the \emph{selection} of the best one (IBE). For our model, it is
precisely \emph{generation} that matters: doxa \emph{proposes} hypotheses, and
the formal system \emph{verifies} them \cite{niiniluoto}.

Abduction is \emph{formalizable} --- but only on condition that we formalize
the entire ``pattern'' of abductive inference, including the rules of
hypothesis generation and justification. There exist formal systems in which
abduction is added to propositional logic as an \emph{external extension
operator} \cite{kakas}. This means that abduction can be described not as an
inference rule inside the logic, but as an operation over the theory that
supplies a new proposal to it.

\subsection{The JSM method: a synthesis of cognitive procedures}
\label{sec:dsm}

Abduction describes the \emph{generation} of a hypothesis. A separate
question is how a hypothesis is \emph{accepted} as a working one if it does not
follow from the theory. A formal approach to this question was proposed by
\emph{V.~K.~Finn} within the framework of the \emph{JSM method for automatic
hypothesis generation} \cite{finn_dsm}. The method is named after John Stuart
Mill and formalizes his inductive methods by means of modern logic.

The key idea of the JSM method is that a cognitive procedure is not reducible
to induction, analogy, or abduction taken separately. It is a \emph{synthesis}
of the three: induction generates hypotheses about causal relations on the
basis of similarity and difference of objects; analogy transfers these
hypotheses to new objects; abduction checks the condition of causal
completeness --- whether the causes found suffice to explain the observed
effect \cite{finn_voprosy, finn_epistemology}.

For our model, the JSM method matters for two reasons. First, it shows that
\emph{generation} and \emph{acceptance} of a hypothesis are different
operations, and the second does not reduce to the first. Second, a hypothesis
in the JSM method receives a \emph{degree of plausibility}, not a binary
status ``true/false,'' which corresponds to the probabilistic nature of
doxa (Section~\ref{sec:intro}). Although mathematically distinct—statistical
frequency in LLMs versus inductive support in JSM—both fulfill the same
epistemic role: providing a graded, non-binary assessment of a hypothesis
prior to its strict verification by logos. The JSM method can be regarded
as yet another formal analogue of an \emph{external} operator: it describes
how a hypothesis enters the theory with an explicitly stated ground.

\subsection{Limits and complexity}
\label{sec:limits_theory}

The four approaches describe different operations over a formal theory.
Doxastic logic and DDL work with an agent's beliefs as a formalized slice;
non-monotonic logics describe the cancellation of conclusions when new
information arrives; abduction supplies a new proposal to the theory; the JSM
method generates hypotheses and assesses their plausibility through a synthesis
of induction, analogy, and abduction. None of them works with doxa as an
object: all four deal with operations, not with the source. It is precisely
these operations that we call \emph{functions of doxa}.

A separate difficulty is associated with these operations. Non-monotonic
logics are computationally more complex than classical logic \cite{vinkov},
and for many of them the entailment problem is undecidable or of very high
complexity \cite{cadoli}: no general algorithm exists that, for any theory and
goal, would determine whether the goal is a consequence. A procedure can be
built, but its termination cannot be guaranteed. Abduction generates a
multitude of hypotheses, and choosing among them is a separate task without a
formal criterion of truth: propositional abduction is $\Sigma_2^P$-complete
\cite{eiter95b}, and logical abduction is computationally hard in the general
case \cite{eiter95}. The JSM method, being a synthesis of three cognitive
procedures, inherits their complexity: checking causal completeness and
assessing the plausibility of hypotheses require enumeration of subsets of
features and in the general case do not reduce to polynomial procedures
\cite{finn_dsm}.

\subsection{Connection to our model}
\label{sec:theory_model}

The functions of doxa --- how it extends a formal system --- we describe
formally. Each of the four approaches above corresponds to one or more
operators, and each operator belongs to one of three roles:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Doxastic logic} $\to$ the \emph{fixation} operator: what
	      the agent ``holds'' as beliefs. This is a \emph{protocol} function
	      of the environment, implemented by the journal.
	\item \textbf{Dynamic doxastic logic} $\to$ the \emph{revision of the
	      formal system} operator. This is an \emph{internal} operator:
	      \emph{logos} computes how the theory changes when new information
	      arrives from doxa.
	\item \textbf{Non-monotonic logics} $\to$ the \emph{cancellation}
	      operator. This is an \emph{internal} operator: \emph{logos}
	      recomputes what follows when exceptions obtained from doxa appear.
	\item \textbf{Abduction and the JSM method} $\to$ the \emph{hypothesis
	      generation} operator. This is an \emph{external} operator: doxa
	      supplies a hypothesis that the formal system verifies. Abduction
	      describes the generation itself; the JSM method adds to it an
	      assessment of plausibility through a synthesis of induction,
	      analogy, and abduction.
\end{itemize}

This is \emph{not} an exhaustive list. We will return to it in
Section~\ref{sec:operators}, where we introduce the \emph{family of interaction
operators between doxa and logos} and formal definitions of the roles. It is
already clear: the distinction between doxa and logos rests on formal analogues
in modern logic, and these analogues can be used to build a working system.

\section{A family of interaction operators between doxa and logos}
\label{sec:operators}

In Section~\ref{sec:theory} we showed that the distinction between doxa and
logos rests on formal analogues in modern logic. Here we introduce the
\emph{family of operators} through which doxa and logos interact. We are
interested not in the content of doxa, but in the \emph{protocol} of its use.

The section is structured as follows. First we give basic definitions and the
criterion by which operators are divided into three classes
(Section~\ref{sec:roles}). Then we describe each class separately: external
operators, internal operators, and protocol operators. After that we introduce
the role of the Arbitrator --- an external instance that belongs neither to
doxa nor to logos. Finally, we formulate properties of the family, describe the
process of reasoning as a sequence of acts of consulting doxa, and clarify the
role of logos in this process.

The central claim of the section is that this protocol is \emph{asymmetric}:
doxa supplies the content of proposals, whereas everything that decides,
records, and controls their fate belongs to logos and the interaction
environment.

\subsection{Basic definitions and the role criterion}
\label{sec:roles}

Let $F = (A, R, L)$ be a formal system with axioms $A$, inference rules $R$,
and language $L$, and let $\mathrm{Cn}(F)$ be the set of its consequences. Let
$D$ be doxa (Section~\ref{sec:intro}): a source of proposals for $F$, which is
not part of it and does not form a theory in $L$.

An operator may extend the \emph{theory} (new axioms, rules, or objects) or
the \emph{language} itself; changing $L$ changes what counts as a formula, and
the two kinds of extension must not be confused. In what follows we consider
only extensions of the theory: the language $L$ is assumed fixed.

Let $\Sigma = \langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ be the state of the system,
where $F$ is the theory, $\mathcal{J}$ is the journal, and $\Pi$ is a sequence
of proposed but not yet accepted proposals. An operator is a function
$o: \Sigma \times X \to \Sigma$, where $X$ is the input (an observation, new
information, a context, or an empty input). Classification is by what exactly
the operator changes in $\Sigma$: the theory, the proposal queue, or the
journal.

We define the \emph{initial state} as $\Sigma_0 = \langle F_0, \emptyset,
\emptyset \rangle$, where $F_0$ contains only initial background knowledge
(or is empty), the journal $\mathcal{J}$ is empty, and the proposal queue
$\Pi$ is empty.

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{External} (doxastic) operator \emph{produces a proposal}:
	      content whose source is doxa. Formally:
	      $o(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, x)
	      = \langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \frown \langle P \rangle \rangle$.
	      The theory and the journal do not change; the proposal enters
	      $\Pi$ and awaits a decision by logos.
	\item \textbf{Internal} (logotic) operator \emph{produces a new theory}:
	      $F'$, computed from $F$ and the input. Formally:
	      $o(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, x)
	      = \langle F', \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$,
	      where $F'$ is a deterministic function of $F$ and $x$ under fixed
	      rules. Neither the journal nor the queue $\Pi$ is changed by the
	      operator: extracting the input from $\Pi$ and recording the act is
	      the work of the protocol operator (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).
	\item \textbf{Protocol} operator \emph{extracts a proposal and produces a
	      record}: it does not change $F$. Formally:
	      $o(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, x)
	      = \langle F, \mathcal{J}', \Pi' \rangle$,
	      where $\mathcal{J}' = \mathcal{J} \cup \{\text{record of the act}\}$,
	      and $\Pi'$ is the queue $\Pi$ without the extracted proposal $x$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{External operators (doxastic)}
\label{sec:external}

External operators change neither the theory $F$ nor the journal
$\mathcal{J}$; they add a proposal to the queue $\Pi$, whence logos later
retrieves it. The record of the act is made by the protocol operator
(Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

\textbf{Hypothesis introduction operator ($o_{\text{abd}}$).} Takes the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and a pair $\langle Q, P \rangle$
proposed by doxa --- an observation $Q$ and a hypothesis $P$ explaining $Q$ ---
and appends $P$ to the end of $\Pi$:
\[
o_{\text{abd}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, \langle Q, P \rangle)
= \langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \frown \langle P \rangle \rangle,
\]
where four conditions hold: $P \notin \mathrm{Cn}(F)$ (the hypothesis is
new), $Q \notin \mathrm{Cn}(F)$ (the observation is not derivable from the
theory and therefore requires explanation), $Q \in \mathrm{Cn}(F \cup \{P\})$
(the hypothesis explains the observation), and $F \cup \{P\}$ is consistent.

The consistency condition is essential: if $F \cup \{P\}$ is inconsistent,
then by the principle of explosion any $Q$ follows from it, and the
explanation condition becomes trivial. The condition $Q \notin \mathrm{Cn}(F)$
is equally essential from the other side: if the observation is already
derivable from $F$, the abductive introduction of $P$ explains nothing.

The explanation condition by itself does not require \emph{minimality} of the
hypothesis: if $P$ explains $Q$, then $P \land R$ also explains $Q$. The
selection of hypotheses without redundant conjuncts is performed at the stage
of protocol classification (Section~\ref{sec:logos_role}).

The theory does not change; the proposal enters $\Pi$ and awaits a decision by
logos. This is the formal analogue of abduction: the hypothesis is
\emph{proposed}, not derived \cite{kakas}.

\textbf{Analogy operator ($o_{\text{anal}}$).} Takes the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and a pair of domains
$\langle F_s, F_t \rangle$ --- a familiar domain $F_s$ and a new domain
$F_t$ --- and adds to $\Pi$ a proposal $P_{\text{analog}}$ transferring the
structure of $F_s$ to $F_t$:
\[
o_{\text{anal}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, \langle F_s, F_t \rangle)
= \langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \frown \langle P_{\text{analog}} \rangle \rangle,
\]
where $P_{\text{analog}} \notin \mathrm{Cn}(F)$. The choice of the structural
mapping is external; logos only checks whether it is preserved.

\textbf{Intuition operator ($o_{\text{int}}$).} Takes the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and a goal $\phi$ and adds to $\Pi$ an
answer $\phi'$ that does not follow from $F$:
\[
o_{\text{int}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, \phi)
= \langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \frown \langle \phi' \rangle \rangle,
\]
where $\phi' \notin \mathrm{Cn}(F)$. This operator is closest to an oracle.
Doxa \emph{gives} the answer; logos verifies it if it can, otherwise defers it
as a hypothesis. What is oracle-like here is the \emph{source} of the answer,
not the bearer, which may well be computable.

\subsection{Internal operators (logotic)}
\label{sec:internal}

These operators are often mistaken for functions of doxa because they are
triggered by its input. In fact it is logos that computes them; the input is
only their argument. Internal operators change the theory $F$ but do not touch
the journal $\mathcal{J}$: the record of the act is made by the protocol
operator (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}).

The input of an internal operator comes from one of two sources. The first is
a \emph{proposal extracted from the queue} $\Pi$ by a protocol operator: the
protocol removes it from $\Pi$, records the act in the journal, and passes the
proposal to the internal operator as an argument. This happens in revision
and cancellation. The second source is an input already present in $F$
(conflicting rules in specificity selection) or \emph{declared by the
protocol} (context); in this case $\Pi$ does not participate. In all cases the
internal operator itself changes only $F$; changing $\Pi$ and $\mathcal{J}$ is
the prerogative of the protocol.

\textbf{Revision operator ($o_{\text{rev}}$).} Given the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and new information $p$ arriving from
the queue $\Pi$, returns $F'$ in which beliefs are revised in light of $p$:
\[
o_{\text{rev}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, p)
= \langle F', \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle,
\]
where $F'$ is a deterministic function of $F$ and $p$ under a fixed selection
function implementing \emph{belief base revision} in the spirit of AGM
principles \cite{agm}. The selection function is considered \emph{part of the
formal system} $F$: it enters its structure alongside axioms and inference
rules and is not a hidden parameter of the state $\Sigma$. Thus $F$ here is a
base of axioms and rules together with a preference ordering, not a logically
closed set of beliefs; hence the talk is of base revision. Doxa supplies $p$;
logos decides how $p$ affects $F$. Extracting $p$ from $\Pi$ and recording the
act is performed by the protocol operator (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}); the
internal operator changes only $F$.

\textbf{Cancellation operator ($o_{\text{def}}$).} Given the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and an exception $e$ arriving from the
queue $\Pi$, returns $F'$ in which previously derived consequences are
cancelled:
\[
o_{\text{def}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, e)
= \langle F', \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle.
\]
This is the formal analogue of non-monotonic logics. Cancellation does not
mean deleting axioms: what was derivable ceases to be derivable under the new
configuration. Extracting $e$ from $\Pi$ and recording the act is performed
by the protocol operator.

\textbf{Specificity operator ($o_{\text{spec}}$).} Given the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and two rules $r_1, r_2$ present in $F$,
returns $F'$ in which the more specific one is selected:
\[
o_{\text{spec}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, \{r_1, r_2\})
= \langle F', \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle.
\]
Specificity is a relation \emph{computed by logos} over the hierarchy of $F$;
doxa does not supply it. This is the formal analogue of specificity preference
in non-monotonic logics. Since the rules are already in $F$, the queue $\Pi$
does not change.

\textbf{Context application operator ($o_{\text{ctx}}$).} Given the state
$\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle$ and a declared context $\Gamma$, returns
$F'$ to which the axioms of the context $\Gamma$ have been added:
\[
o_{\text{ctx}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, \Gamma)
= \langle F \cup \Gamma, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle.
\]
The context is \emph{declared} by the protocol; its \emph{application} is
internal. Since $\Gamma$ is not a proposal from the queue, $\Pi$ does not
change.

\subsection{Protocol operators (environment)}
\label{sec:protocol}

These functions belong neither to doxa nor to logos. They introduce no content
and do not change the theory $F$; they change only the journal $\mathcal{J}$
and thereby make the exchange well-defined, deterministic, and verifiable.

Strictly speaking, only fixation is an operator in the sense of
Section~\ref{sec:roles}: it has the form $o: \Sigma \times X \to \Sigma$. The
other protocol functions --- classification, declaration, grounding checks,
the wave protocol --- are rules and procedures specifying how exactly the
record is made. The general form of the operator part is:
\[
o_{\text{fix}}(\langle F, \mathcal{J}, \Pi \rangle, x)
= \langle F, \mathcal{J}', \Pi \rangle,
\quad \mathcal{J}' = \mathcal{J} \cup \{\text{record of the act}\}.
\]

\textbf{Journal and fixation ($o_{\text{fix}}$).} Each step is recorded in the
journal: operator identifier, wave, rationale, source, and result (accepted,
rejected, deferred). The journal is the protocol of interaction, not part of
either doxa or logos. Fixation means selecting the formalized slice of doxa
that is being consulted, and recording this act; it is an accounting action of
the environment, not a function of doxa.

\textbf{Deterministic classification.} Every proposal from $\Pi$ is classified
by fixed rules (Section~\ref{sec:logos_role}), independently of ``mood''; this
is what makes the exchange reproducible.

\textbf{Declaration of context and fragment.} The context $\Gamma$ or the
required logic fragment is \emph{declared} as a run parameter; its application
is internal (see $o_{\text{ctx}}$ above).

\textbf{Grounding checks.} Checks of verbatim quotations and other structural
checks certify that a proposal is anchored in the source.

\textbf{Wave protocol.} The rule ``at most one proposal per act of
consultation'' makes each act verifiable and leaves a trace of where and why
the theory changed.

\subsection{Acceptance of proposals}
\label{sec:acceptance}

The set $\Pi$ contains proposals awaiting a decision. The decision is made by
logos through protocol classification (Section~\ref{sec:logos_role}). Three
outcomes are possible.

\textbf{Accepted.} The protocol extracts the proposal from $\Pi$ and passes it
to the corresponding internal operator. The latter applies the proposal to
$F$; if the proposal rests on a quotation from the source, it enters $F$ as an
axiom; if it is an assumption, it enters with the mark $H$ and is taken into
account in assessing the strength of the answer. The protocol records the act
in the journal $\mathcal{J}$.

\textbf{Rejected.} The protocol extracts the proposal from $\Pi$, does not
pass it to the internal operator, and records the refusal in the journal with
a reason code. The theory $F$ does not change.

\textbf{Deferred.} The proposal remains in $\Pi$ until the next wave or until
the budget is exhausted.

In all three cases the protocol is responsible for extracting the proposal
from $\Pi$ and for the record in $\mathcal{J}$, while the internal operator is
responsible for changing $F$. The journal stores the history: which proposal
was considered and what decision was made. $\Pi$ itself stores no history: it
contains only awaiting proposals.

\subsection{The Arbitrator}
\label{sec:arbiter}

So far we have described operators that supply, compute, and record. But a
question remains: who decides when the process stops, which of the competing
proposals of doxa is accepted, and whether the goal has been reached? This
question requires a separate role.

\textbf{The Arbitrator} is an external instance that belongs neither to doxa
nor to logos, but for which the system exists. The Arbitrator produces no
content and computes no reaction of logos; it \emph{selects} which operator
from the family to apply to the current state.

The role of the Arbitrator is modeled as an external, with respect to the
system, selection function $W: \Sigma \to \mathcal{O}$, where $\Sigma$ is the
state of the system and $\mathcal{O}$ is the set of operators of the family.
The type of the function is given, but its algorithmic implementation lies
outside the formal model $\Sigma$ --- otherwise we would have infinite regress
(the Arbitrator would itself be subject to verification, and a meta-Arbitrator
would be required).

The Arbitrator may be:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a \emph{human} --- a researcher, user, or expert who makes decisions
          where a formal criterion is absent or insufficient;
    \item an \emph{external program} --- a test runner, verifier, or oracle
          that returns an objective verdict (for example, whether a test
          passed);
    \item a \emph{composition} --- for example, an automatic arbitrator makes
          decisions in known cases, and a human is involved only when the
          automatic one abstains.
\end{itemize}

The model \emph{deliberately does not automate} the Arbitrator's step fully.
No formal criterion resolves a dispute between incompatible proposals of doxa
if both are compatible with the theory. The choice remains with the one who is
responsible for it --- a human or an external program acting by a fixed
criterion.

\subsection{Properties of the family}
\label{sec:properties}

External operators do not commute, and the order of their application matters.
Let us examine this with an example. Let the theory $F$ contain
\texttt{is\_a(tweety, bird)} and the default
\texttt{bird} $\Rightarrow$ \texttt{fly}, and let doxa propose
\texttt{is\_a(tweety, penguin)}.

\textbf{First order:} first $o_{\text{abd}}$, then acceptance of the
proposal, then $o_{\text{spec}}$. After $o_{\text{abd}}$ the proposal
\texttt{is\_a(tweety, penguin)} enters $\Pi$ and awaits a decision. If logos
accepts it, it enters $F$, and a more specific default
\texttt{penguin} $\Rightarrow$ $\neg$\,\texttt{fly} appears in the theory.
The operator $o_{\text{spec}}$ selects it, and \texttt{fly} is cancelled.

\textbf{Second order:} first $o_{\text{spec}}$, then $o_{\text{abd}}$. The
theory still has one default (\texttt{bird} $\Rightarrow$ \texttt{fly}), there
is no competitor, and \texttt{fly} is derived. Only after that does
$o_{\text{abd}}$ propose \texttt{is\_a(tweety, penguin)}. Now, if the proposal
is accepted, a second default appears in the theory, conflicting with the
already drawn conclusion. This forces not a specificity selection but a
\emph{revision}: the previously derived consequence is reconsidered.

The same operators in a different order yield different theories: the process
is historical, and each step changes the space of possible next steps.

The family does not reduce to a single operator. The expression
$o_1 \cup o_2$ is not an operator: since the effect of $o_2$ depends on
whether $o_1$ has already been applied, there is no function of the initial
state $\Sigma$ alone that would capture this pair.

External operators do not guarantee a result. $o_{\text{abd}}$ may propose a
contradictory hypothesis, $o_{\text{anal}}$ an unsuitable mapping,
$o_{\text{int}}$ an erroneous answer. This is not a defect but the nature of
doxa: it proposes, but does not guarantee. Internal operators, by contrast,
are deterministic functions of $F$ and their input; protocol operators
guarantee determinism and verifiability, not truth.

We also note that composition of operators is not always defined: some pairs
of operators are incompatible (for example, two operators each of which
requires monopolistic access to $\Pi$). Idempotence is absent: repeated
application of $o_{\text{abd}}$ may generate a new hypothesis. Reversibility
is also absent: cancelling a hypothesis changes the theory $F$, but the record
of it remains in the journal $\mathcal{J}$. A full study of the algebraic
properties of the family is the subject of a separate paper; here we fix only
those properties that are essential for what follows.

\subsection{The process of reasoning}
\label{sec:process}

Reasoning is a \emph{sequence} of extensions:
\[
	\Sigma_0 \xrightarrow{o_1} \Sigma_1 \xrightarrow{o_2} \Sigma_2 \xrightarrow{o_3} \dots
\]
where each step is an \emph{act} of consulting doxa, marked by the operator
that mediates it; fixation, classification, and application of each act are
performed by the protocol. Not a ``computation'', but a \emph{consultation} of
the source.

Each step is \emph{recorded} in the \emph{journal}: operator identifier, wave,
rationale, source, and result (accepted / rejected / deferred). The journal is
the \emph{protocol} of the interaction between doxa and logos. It \emph{is not}
part of either doxa or logos. It is an \emph{interface}, a protocol operator,
not a function of doxa.

\subsection{Limits of the model}
\label{sec:model_limits}

The list of operators is \emph{open}: the model does not claim that all have
been identified. There may exist operators we do not see, and aspects of doxa
that are not expressed through operators. This is not a temporary limitation
but a consequence of the subject matter: doxa is not a theory in $L$, and its
interaction with logos is described precisely by those functions that have
been identified.

\subsection{The role of logos}
\label{sec:logos_role}

Logos is \emph{not passive}. It \emph{classifies} every proposal of doxa:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{derivable} --- the proposal already follows from $F$.
	      Accepted as \emph{narrative}.
	\item \textbf{cited} --- the proposal is grounded in a \emph{quotation}
	      from the text. Added to the axioms.
	\item \textbf{hypothesis} --- the proposal is new. Recorded in the
	      journal with the mark $H$.
	\item \textbf{rejected} --- the proposal fails verification. Discarded
	      with a reason code.
\end{itemize}

Classification is a protocol function; the verdict it issues belongs to logos.
Classification is \emph{deterministic}: it depends on the \emph{structure} of
$F$ and the \emph{content} of the proposal, not on the ``mood'' of logos. This
is the \emph{guarantee} that logos does not ``yield'' to doxa. It
\emph{accepts} only what it \emph{can} accept. And it \emph{honestly} says when
it \emph{cannot}.

\section{Implementation: Ankyra}
\label{sec:implementation}

In Section~\ref{sec:operators} we introduced the family of interaction
operators between doxa and logos. Here we show that this model is not a
theoretical construct: it is implemented in a working system, the hybrid
neuro-symbolic reasoning system Ankyra \cite{ankyra}. We describe how each of
the three roles is implemented --- external operators, internal operators, and
the protocol --- and note that a full description of the architecture and its
empirical evaluation is the subject of a separate paper
\cite{ankyra_paper}.

\subsection{Doxa is the LLM}
\label{sec:llm_doxa}

What exactly the LLM brings into the system in the role of doxa. It is trained
on texts, and text is not merely a means of transmitting information but a
picture of the world: grammar, lexicon, and metaphors set the grid through
which reality is reconstructed. The LLM is a hypertrophied version of that
grid. It carries compressed human experience: the cultural field accessible
through prediction of the next token; historicity frozen in probabilities.
This is what makes it a bearer of doxa --- not computability and not
architecture, but the fact that it is a \emph{cast} of human language and
experience.

\subsection{The principle: the LLM proposes, the engine decides}
\label{sec:principle}

The core of Ankyra is the distinction between two participants. The LLM plays
the role of doxa: it reads the text, decomposes it into structure, and in the
reasoning cycle proposes additional steps. The symbolic engine plays the role
of logos: it turns the structure into a formal theory, closes it under
inference, matches it against the question, and certifies what actually
follows.

The boundary is absolute. The LLM never possesses truth. Nothing enters the
theory that the engine cannot justify either by a verbatim quotation from the
text or by an explicit hypothesis mark.

\subsection{The proposal contract}
\label{sec:contract}

Every statement the LLM wishes to add is deterministically classified into one
of four categories: \texttt{derivable} (already follows from the theory),
\texttt{cited} (supported by a verbatim quotation), \texttt{hypothesis}
(knowledge not present in the text), \texttt{rejected} (fails verification).
This is a direct implementation of the hypothesis generation operator
($o_{\text{abd}}$): the LLM proposes, the engine classifies, nothing is
accepted without grounds. The distinction between \texttt{cited} and
\texttt{hypothesis} makes the answers honest: a conclusion based on quotations
is \texttt{proven}, a conclusion based on assumptions is
\texttt{proven\_under(H)} with a listing of those assumptions. An answer is
never stronger than the grounds behind it.

\subsection{The reasoning cycle}
\label{sec:cycle}

A task passes through four phases: \emph{phase 0} --- decomposition (the LLM
decomposes the text into structure), \emph{phase 1} --- solving (the engine
closes the theory), \emph{phase 2} --- the hint cycle (the LLM proposes one
step at a time if the theory does not solve the question), \emph{phase 3} ---
explanation (the chain is assembled mechanically from the proof history).

The hint cycle (phase 2) is organized into \emph{waves}. A wave is one
complete act of consulting doxa: the engine checks the current state of the
theory and formulates the gaps, the LLM receives a hint with these gaps and
proposes \emph{exactly one} typed proposal, the engine classifies this
proposal and applies or discards it, after which the wave is recorded in the
journal and the cycle begins anew. The constraint ``one proposal per wave'' is
principled: it makes each consultation of doxa verifiable and leaves a trace
by which one can reconstruct at which step and why the answer changed.

The hint cycle implements the process of consulting doxa from
Section~\ref{sec:operators}: each wave is one act
$F_i \xrightarrow{o} F_{i+1}$, mediated by an \emph{external} operator,
whereas fixation, classification, and application are performed by the
protocol.

\subsection{The journal}
\label{sec:journal}

Each step is recorded in the journal: operator identifier, wave, rationale,
source, result. The journal is the protocol of interaction between doxa and
logos, not part of either. It implements the \emph{protocol} role of the
family (journal and fixation): it records each act of consulting doxa and
makes the process verifiable and reproducible. This is not a function of doxa.

\subsection{Monotone base and moving answer}
\label{sec:revision}

The base --- axioms, rules, hypotheses --- only grows: premises are never
deleted, the goal is never weakened. But the answer may change as new
information arrives. Each change is recorded as a \texttt{Revision} with an
indication of the wave and the assumptions involved. This is an
\emph{internal} operator of the family (revision, $o_{\text{rev}}$): doxa
supplies information, logos computes how it affects the theory, and the
decision is recorded, not hidden.

\subsection{Engines and the declared fragment}
\label{sec:engines}

Ankyra is not a monolithic solver but an orchestrator of formalizations and
decision procedures. Different kinds of tasks require different logics: Horn
clauses (L0), stratified negation (L1), disjunction and quantifiers (L2),
finite-domain constraints (L3), arithmetic (L4), defaults with specificity
(D). These engines are \emph{internal} operators of the family (the logotic
side): L1 and D implement, respectively, cancellation ($o_{\text{def}}$) and
specificity ($o_{\text{spec}}$), while L2 implements case analysis inside the
decision procedure. None of them is a source of content.

The choice of procedure is carried out through the contract of the declared
fragment: the engine examines the constructed theory and query and derives the
set of required features. If the fragment fits within the available
capabilities, the corresponding procedure is chosen; if not, the result is the
named gap \texttt{out\_of\_fragment}, rather than a silent downgrade to a
weaker logic. The declaration of the fragment is a \emph{protocol} function;
its application is internal, and the system honestly reports a mismatch rather
than guessing.

\subsection{The Arbitrator in the implementation}
\label{sec:arbiter_impl}

In Ankyra, the role of the Arbitrator is divided. The automatic Arbitrator is
a \emph{test runner}: it returns an objective verdict (the test passed or not)
and does not depend on the proposals of doxa. The user Arbitrator is a
\emph{human} who accepts tasks, sets goals, and decides when the process is
complete. The automatic Arbitrator is involved in phase 1 (when the goal is
checked) and in phase 2 (when a proposal of doxa changes the theory); the user
Arbitrator is involved in the external interface of the system. A full
description of the Arbitrator's role in the architecture is the subject of a
separate paper \cite{ankyra_paper}.

\subsection{Honesty by construction}
\label{sec:honesty}

The value of the system rests on guarantees provided mechanically: there are
no facts in the theory belonging to the model; the question is never a source;
there is no natural-language parsing in deterministic code; having assumed
\texttt{P}, one cannot refute \texttt{$\neg$P}; an answer that used
assumptions is always marked \texttt{proven\_under(H)}. This is the
implementation of the transparency property of the interface: logos accepts
only what it can accept, and honestly says when it cannot.

\subsection{Connection to the conceptual model}
\label{sec:model_link}

Ankyra implements the three roles of the family from
Section~\ref{sec:operators}. \emph{External}: the proposal contract
(hypothesis introduction operator, $o_{\text{abd}}$). \emph{Internal}:
revision (accounting of \texttt{Revision}), cancellation (L1), specificity
(D). \emph{Protocol}: journal, deterministic classification, grounding checks
of quotations, wave protocol, and declaration of the fragment. The role of the
\emph{Arbitrator} is implemented as a composition of an automatic (test
runner) and a user (human) component. Two external operators are not yet
implemented: analogy ($o_{\text{anal}}$) and intuition ($o_{\text{int}}$). The
system does not claim completeness: it implements what it can implement
honestly, and honestly says what it cannot.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We have introduced the distinction between doxa and logos, shown its formal
analogues, and proposed the family of interaction operators between doxa and
logos, implemented in Ankyra. Now we must discuss the limits of the approach:
what it explains, what it does not explain, and what consequences follow from
it.

\subsection{What the model explains}
\label{sec:explains}

The model shows how a formal system can expand. G\"odel's and Turing's
theorems describe the limits of closed systems. Our model shows that an open
system --- one that interacts with doxa through an explicit interface --- can
expand without that expansion being a derivation within it. The expansion
comes from outside. Logos does not produce it. It accepts, rejects, or defers
it.

Incompleteness is not eliminated but \emph{transferred}: the resulting theory
$F'$ is again incomplete if it is consistent and sufficiently rich, but the
point at which the system stops has shifted from closure to the interface. The
system continues to work where a closed system would have stopped.

\subsection{Limits of the approach}
\label{sec:limits_approach}

The model works with the functions of interaction, not with the content of
doxa. This is a boundary of the subject matter, not a temporary limitation:
doxa is not a theory in the language $L$, and there is nothing to describe in
it --- except those operations through which it extends a formal system. These
operations are what the model is about.

The model also does not guarantee that the set of operators we have identified
is complete. We regard it as an open list. The operators of analogy and
intuition, not implemented in the current version, show that the space of
operators is wider than what we have described. There may exist operators we
do not see. This is not a deficiency of the model but a consequence of its
subject matter.

Finally, the model does not provide a criterion for the ``correct'' choice
among competing proposals of doxa. If two operators generate incompatible
hypotheses, the system records the conflict but does not resolve it
automatically. The choice remains with the \emph{Arbitrator}
(Section~\ref{sec:operators}) --- a human or an external program acting by a
declared criterion. The model deliberately does not automate this step: no
formal criterion resolves a dispute between incompatible proposals of doxa if
both are compatible with the theory.

\subsection{Connection to the oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}

In computation theory, the notion of an oracle was introduced by Turing to
describe a machine that can obtain answers to questions not solvable by its
own procedure \cite{turing39}. The oracle is not part of the machine, but the
machine can consult it. Doxa in our model plays a structurally analogous role.

There is, however, an important difference. The oracle gives \emph{true}
answers to questions of a certain class. Doxa does not give true answers: it
gives \emph{proposals} that may be true, false, or indeterminate. This is
precisely why the interface to doxa includes classification --- a proposal
enters the system not as truth but as a hypothesis requiring verification.
Logos does not trust doxa. It uses it.

\subsection{Practical consequences}
\label{sec:practice}

For explainable artificial intelligence, the model gives one clear principle:
\emph{an answer must not be stronger than its grounds}. If the system derived
a conclusion from quotations --- that is one thing. If from assumptions ---
another. If from a mixture --- a third, and this third must be explicitly
marked. The user has the right to know what the answer rests on.

For system verification, this means that verifiability does not require full
formalization. It is enough that each step is traceable to a source and that
each extension is recorded in the journal. Doxa remains non-formalizable, but
its \emph{trace} in the system is formal.

For the architecture of hybrid systems, this means that the division between
the neural and symbolic parts is not an engineering compromise. It is a
structural division reflecting the distinction between doxa and logos. The
neural part is not ``weaker'' than the symbolic. It does what the symbolic
cannot do. The symbolic part is not ``stricter'' than the neural. It does what
the neural must not do. Each side is strong in its own way.

\subsection{What remains open}
\label{sec:open}

The model leaves several questions open. How do operators of different roles
relate to one another --- are there laws linking external, internal, and
protocol steps, identities, priorities? How does one measure the effectiveness
of an operator --- by the depth of change in the theory, by the number of
accepted proposals, by the contribution to the final answer? How does one
distinguish an operator that genuinely extends the system from one that merely
reformulates what is already known? These questions have no answer within the
present work. They point to the direction of further research.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Map of the field}
\label{sec:map}

A 2026 systematic survey \cite{survey2026} (178 works, 2020--2025) identifies
four stable patterns of neuro-symbolic integration: sequential cascade,
symbolic validator, symbolic planner with a neural executor, and
differentiable integration. Ankyra is closest to the sequential cascade with a
bounded cycle, but it differs in two respects: a proposal protocol under which
every statement enters the theory only with a quotation or an assumption tag,
and a declared fragment under which a mismatch between the required and
available logic yields an honest \texttt{out\_of\_fragment} rather than a
silent downgrade to a weaker procedure. The same survey notes that
meta-cognitive abilities --- monitoring one's own conclusions, recognizing a
lack of knowledge, switching modes --- appear in only 5\% of the works;
\texttt{out\_of\_fragment} and \texttt{proven\_under(H)} can be seen as a
contribution to exactly this niche.

The closest system in spirit is \emph{Autonomous Trustworthy Agents} (ATA)
\cite{ata}: it also pursues determinism and checkability, but achieves them by
\emph{removing} the LLM from the runtime entirely (the LLM builds a knowledge
base offline; only the symbolic solver runs online). Ankyra keeps the LLM in
the loop, constraining it through the protocol; the two are different points
on the same spectrum. The same approach to verifying regulatory documents with
an SMT solver is described in \cite{nsc}.

\subsection{Language model and symbolic solver}
\label{sec:translator_solver}

Translator-plus-solver pipelines (Logic-LM \cite{logiclm}, LINC \cite{linc})
also hand reasoning over to a symbolic solver, but trust the translation
entirely: if the formalization is wrong, so is the conclusion. In our model
the boundary is stricter: every statement enters the theory only with a
verbatim quotation or an explicit assumption tag, answer strength is derived
from the premises (\texttt{proven} versus \texttt{proven\_under}), and a
mismatch between the required and available logic yields an honest
\texttt{out\_of\_fragment} rather than a hidden downgrade to a weaker
procedure. An empirical comparison of Ankyra with Logic-LM and LINC on common
input is given in the companion paper \cite{ankyra_paper}.

\subsection{Non-monotonic reasoning and defaults}
\label{sec:nonmono_related}

Default logic \cite{reiter} and circumscription \cite{mccarthy} formalize the
defeasibility of conclusions; Ankyra implements a defaults layer with
specificity via \texttt{is\_a} and honestly reports an undecided conflict
rather than guessing a winner. The philosophical framing (doxa/logos)
introduced in this paper differs from purely engineering approaches to
separating the LLM and the solver: it derives an architectural principle from
an epistemological distinction going back to the ancient tradition.

\subsection{Proof history and checkability}
\label{sec:proof_history}

The reliance on an automatically assembled proof history aligns Ankyra with
work on checkable proof. The explanation here is a presentation of the
derivation, not a second opinion about it: the model may not add facts to it,
and every step names the rule that fired, the bindings used, and the
supporting quotation or hypothesis.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have proposed an interpretation of the incompleteness theorems not as
absolute limits of cognition but as limits of static systems. Human thinking
is not a static system: it develops by relying on doxa --- a non-formalizable
source of lived experience, the cultural field, and bodily knowledge. The
distinction between doxa and logos has formal analogues in modern logic, but
none of them formalizes doxa as such.

Our contribution consists in the family of interaction operators between doxa
and logos --- a formal interface between a source of proposals and a formal
system --- and in the fact that this interface is \emph{asymmetric}: external
operators supply content, internal ones decide, and protocol ones make the
exchange verifiable. We have implemented this model in the Ankyra system,
where a large language model plays the role of doxa and a symbolic solver
plays the role of logos \cite{ankyra}. The principle ``the LLM proposes, the
engine decides'' ensures transparency: nothing enters the theory without
grounds, and an answer is never stronger than the grounds behind it.

We do not claim to have solved the problem of incompleteness. We claim to have
shown how a system can work with this problem without attempting to overcome
it. Doxa does not cancel logos --- it nourishes it. And in this interaction
there is not a defeat of formalization but its extension.
